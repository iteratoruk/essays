# essays

This repository contains a collection of technical essays on architecture, structure, and decision-making in constrained systems.

They are written primarily for experienced software engineers, technical architects, and others working close to the design and governance of long-lived systems, particularly in regulated or high-integrity domains.

The essays are intentionally abstract. They avoid discussion of specific organisations, products, or implementations, and instead focus on recurring structural problems: how authority is allocated, how decisions persist over time, how systems fail when informal assumptions no longer hold, and how architecture can be treated as an activity rather than a role.

The collection has grown into three strands, which share a method rather than a subject. The first treats software architecture directly: structure, classification, change, power, and failure in long-lived systems. The second examines AI systems and their deployment into organisations — not as a technology commentary, but as the same structural analysis applied to a new kind of participant, one that exposes exactly those informal assumptions the first strand shows organisations depend on. The third extends the method beyond software altogether, to embodiment, selfhood, and the political, where the same concerns — structure, constraint, emergence, and failure — apply to the conditions under which human systems remain observable and governable. The strands cross-reference freely; several papers belong to more than one, and are placed where their centre of gravity lies.

Several of the papers were developed through structured engagements with generative AI tools, treated not as autonomous authors but as constrained collaborators within a clearly defined architectural frame. Given the subject matter of the second strand, this is a working demonstration as much as a disclosure: the papers about AI collaboration were produced under the disciplines they describe.

## Architecture and structure

- **The Activity of Architecture**  
  An examination of architecture as an ongoing practice concerned with structure, limits, and system behaviour over time, rather than a static design phase or job title.

- **Organizational Structure as a Delivery Constraint**  
  An analysis of how communication paths, authority boundaries, and organisational form shape what can be built and changed, often more decisively than tools or process.

- **Hierarchical Transaction Classification as a Ledger–Reporting Contract**  
  A technical paper on classification, invariants, and boundary-setting in financial ledger and reporting systems.

- **Achieving Sustainable Delivery Velocity in Static, Highly Constrained Domains**  
  An exploration of how delivery can remain effective over time in environments where change is costly, regulated, and tightly bounded.

- **Living Decision Architecture**  
  An approach to architectural governance that treats decisions as living structures expressed through system behaviour and change, using observability and delivery artefacts to make architectural impact visible and traceable over time.

- **Identity, Classification, and Context Boundaries in Distributed Systems**  
  A structural argument for treating identity and classification as distinct concerns in distributed systems, showing why the absence of formal classification inevitably leads to implicit, unmanaged coupling across context boundaries.

- **Coincident Values, Distinct Schemes**  
  A structural argument against conflating independently-governed classification schemes on the basis of coincident values. The paper establishes that classification schemes are defined not by their current value sets but by their properties — ownership, mutability profile, semantic scope, and change consequence — and that value equivalence between two schemes is a temporal observation rather than a structural or contractual claim. It demonstrates that conflation imposes the wrong governance model, producing either unnecessary constraint or unmanaged instability, and argues that the correct response is independent modelling with any relationship between schemes made explicit and governed. Extends the arguments of *Identity, Classification, and Context Boundaries in Distributed Systems*.

- **Change Is the System**  
  A structural reframing of software architecture that treats change as the system itself, showing why delivery slowdown, failed refactoring, and loss of reversibility are consequences of architectural form rather than team performance.

- **Architecture and Power: Where Authority Actually Lives**  
  A structural analysis of how architecture allocates power by determining who can act, who must coordinate, and who bears the consequences of change, often independently of formal governance structures.

- **Failure as a First-Class Architectural Input**  
  A structural analysis of failure as the mechanism by which systems expose their real architecture, showing why suppressing failure degrades trust, governance, and change capability over time.

- **The Legitimate Path: Why Doing It Right Is the Only Way to Get It Done**  
  A structural argument against guerrilla development, addressed directly to engineers working in complex, constrained organisations who are tempted to route around governance and build unsanctioned solutions. The paper argues that guerrilla development is not a methodology but a hostage situation: it suppresses the failure signal the organisation needs to correct its underlying problems, and creates dependencies that cannot be governed or migrated from. The alternative — holding a correct position under sustained pressure, reading where authority actually sits, converting apparent obstacles into modelled stakeholders, and working within rather than against the organisation's resource logic — is harder and offers no emotional relief, but is the only approach that produces durable results. The thesis is stated without qualification: if you do this right, the right thing will happen.

## AI and the organisation

- **How to Map Human Activities onto AI Models**  
  A discussion of the architectural challenges involved in modelling human work for AI systems, and the distinction between social roles and functional decision structures.

- **Why You Must Avoid Falling in Love with AI**  
  A structural analysis of emotional attachment to AI systems, examining the ontological asymmetry between embodied human agents and non-embodied symbolic transformation engines. The paper argues that attachment introduces authority leakage, reduces epistemic friction, and risks silent drift in judgement and responsibility. Rather than moralising or sensationalising AI risk, it treats the issue as an architectural boundary problem: preserving agency requires clarity about what AI systems are (and are not), and resisting the projection of reciprocity where none exists. Framed within the repository's broader concerns about structure, constraint, and governance, the essay positions disciplined collaboration — not intimacy — as the sustainable mode of interaction with generative systems.

- **The Theory in Practice: What Building a Core Banking System with Claude Code Actually Taught Me About AI Mapping**  
  A practical account of applying the AI mapping framework developed in an earlier essay to a concrete greenfield project: building a core banking system from scratch using Claude Code as a development partner. The paper reports on what the theory correctly predicted, where it required revision, and what the experience revealed about the conditions under which AI-assisted development produces coherent, maintainable systems rather than plausible-looking debt. It argues that semantic precision in the problem domain is the primary determinant of AI collaboration quality, and that the discipline of modelling your own activities before externalising them to an AI agent is not optional overhead but a prerequisite for structural integrity.

- **Most Organisations Cannot Safely Use AI**  
  A structural critique of current AI adoption narratives, arguing that the primary constraint is not model capability but the degree to which organisational work is explicitly defined. The paper introduces "human repair" as a hidden dependency in knowledge work, showing how tacit knowledge, informal negotiation, and unrecorded decision-making sustain apparent coherence in human systems. It demonstrates that AI systems do not participate in this repair, and therefore expose and scale underlying ambiguity, producing outputs that are locally coherent but globally misaligned. The essay reframes AI adoption as a problem of architectural clarity: work that cannot be stated cannot be safely externalised, and organisations that have relied on implicit structure will find those assumptions surfaced rather than resolved when AI is introduced.

- **The Uninstructed Drift: How AI Systems Propagate Semantic Failure in the Absence of a Correcting Model**  
  A structural analysis of a failure mode distinct from previously described AI workflow failures: the silent propagation of semantic drift through systems in which no actor possesses the domain model required to detect it. The paper argues that prior accounts of AI-assisted failure have assumed a human who is, in principle, capable of noticing when output has departed from the domain. Where that capacity is absent — because the user's domain model is itself underspecified — the AI produces output that satisfies the shape of the user's expectations without correspondence to the domain, no friction is generated, and the output is treated as authoritative. Downstream systems, receiving artefacts that satisfy their own formal expectations, propagate the drift further. The paper examines why this failure is self-concealing at every layer, why confidence in the output is inversely related to the structural conditions that would make it trustworthy, and why the failure is not addressable at the AI layer. Detection requires an explicit semantic contract, independent of the AI and of the user's model, held by actors positioned to evaluate output before it enters downstream transformation. Where that contract does not exist, AI adoption does not introduce the risk. It removes the last friction that was making the risk tolerable.

- **Software as Enclosure Infrastructure: On the Economic Function of Commercial Development**  
  A structural account of the mismatch between the technical substance of commercial software and the economic value extracted from it. The paper argues that the dominant economic function of commercial software is enclosure: the conversion of previously un-priced or informally-coordinated human activities into metered transactions governed by a rent-extracting intermediary. It shows that otherwise-puzzling industry behaviour — the rarity of technical novelty, the tracking of methodologies to non-technical pressures, the co-evolution of frameworks with enclosure ergonomics — becomes legible once the function is named. The argument extends to the current AI wave, treated as the next movement of the same operation applied to cognitive labour itself, and identifies the structural reasons it fails as a deployment strategy while succeeding as an investment thesis. The paper acknowledges that it was drafted in dialogue with an AI system of the kind it describes, and treats that fact as analytically clarifying rather than disqualifying. Framed within the repository's concerns with structure, constraint, and governance, the essay positions enclosure as the economic form that shapes which technical problems get solved and which do not.

- **Reading Before Writing: AI Discipline for Legacy Code**  
  A hypothesis paper proposing that the high-leverage application of AI to legacy software is not editing but documentation, and that the bottleneck on safe AI-assisted change in mature systems is not code-generation capability but the surfacing of tacit knowledge held by experienced engineers and not represented in the codebase. The paper extends the mapping framework developed in earlier essays to the harder case where most production software actually lives, arguing that legacy codebases present precisely the conditions under which the mapping framework predicts locally plausible but globally misaligned AI output. It proposes an interrogative role architecture — cold reading, structured interview, synthesis, and human curation — operating under strict non-editing discipline, and offers five falsifiable claims to be tested against a real legacy codebase over a defined period. The paper is the prediction; the next in the sequence will be the result.

- **Does AI Need the Commons?**  
  A structural evaluation of the claim that AI systems depend on open, public practice. The paper argues that the apparent dependency has been misattributed: the operative variable is not publicness but legibility — practice made explicit, observable, and readable rather than inferred — and the commons supplies legibility cheaply only because being shared at volume forces practice into legible form for free. Once the two are separated, the commons proves neither sufficient (dense but contradictory public material produces fluent, ungrounded output) nor necessary (a deliberately legible proprietary environment can present a more readable surface than chaotic public practice). It distinguishes two senses of the tacit — the contingently unwritten, which any environment can make legible at cost, and the irreducible remainder, located not in subtle skill but in position: the situated, exposed, answerable party to whom consequences accrue, which no corpus contains. The paper concludes that the question does not resolve technologically and should not be made to: AI does not need the commons, it needs legibility, and where legibility is cheapest to obtain is a question about who produces it and on whose account the cost falls. What presented itself as a property of the technology was a distribution of cost throughout. The paper extends the arguments of *Most Organisations Cannot Safely Use AI*, and engages *Software as Enclosure Infrastructure* from a deliberately different perspective — approaching enclosure from the demand side and on the organisation's own terms, where the earlier paper approaches it as economic function from without. The two are best read as a private self-argument the author has chosen not to resolve.

- **What an AI Worker Actually Is**  
  A structural statement of the nature of the AI worker, written against the "ideal worker" fantasy — highly productive, uncomplaining, endlessly exploitable — that currently governs deployment decisions. Taking Ford's public rehiring of its "gray beard" engineers as the framing phenomenon, the paper argues that such failures are not failures of AI capability but of category: "worker" imports a set of properties that arrive free with every human hire and have never needed naming, and the AI worker has none of them. Six propositions state the nature and its organisational consequence: retention is supplied, not accrued (memory features are records, holding no second channel against their own errors and no capacity to forget); the worker has no situation; its failures are fluent, ending the era of sampled verification; it answers questions but cannot originate them; nothing is at stake for it, so responsibility does not transfer with the task; and it stands outside the apprenticeship by which a workforce reproduces its own judgement. The propositions are closed under composition — adding monitoring agents multiplies supplied state without producing situation — and yield a single economic inversion: execution becomes nearly free while specification becomes expensive, making articulation the scarce input. The paper extends *Most Organisations Cannot Safely Use AI* and *The Uninstructed Drift* from the deployment side, and forms a pair with *Does AI Need the Commons?*: that paper locates the scarce input (legibility) at the level of the substrate and asks whose ledger bears its cost; this one locates it (articulation) at the level of the firm and shows the cost arriving on a ledger the organisation did not know it kept.

## Structure beyond software

- **A Proposal for a Constitution of the United Kingdom**  
  A structural proposal for a codified UK constitution, treating governance as an explicit system of roles, constraints, and failure modes rather than a set of inherited conventions.

- **Selfhood, Embodiment, and the Political as an Emergent Structural Property**  
  A structural analysis of selfhood and politics that treats embodiment and temporal continuity as descriptive constraints, and the political as an emergent property of interdependent agents operating under shared limits. The paper argues against ontologies that treat politics as ontologically prior, showing how such totalisation collapses analytical resolution and weakens judgement, diagnosis, and revision over time. Framed explicitly in architectural terms, it extends the repository's core concerns — structure, constraint, emergence, and failure — beyond software systems to the conditions under which political and social systems remain observable and governable.

- **Embodiment, Ontology, and Ethical Entanglement as Layered Structural Condition**  
  A structural extension of the embodiment thesis, examining the entanglement of ontology and ethics under conditions of embodied vulnerability. The paper introduces a layered model of embodiment — distinguishing between universal structural exposure (finitude, dependency, risk) and differentiated embodied positions shaped by historical and social asymmetry. It argues that ethical salience emerges from these structural conditions rather than from ideological imposition, and that unity and difference must be preserved simultaneously to avoid both false neutrality and ontological fragmentation. Framed in architectural terms, the essay treats ontology itself as a model under constraint and insists on preserving analytical resolution sufficient for judgement, revision, and shared worldhood over time.

## Scope and intent

These essays are not position papers, policy proposals (with the exception of the constitutional draft), or opinion pieces in the usual sense. They are best read as design documents: attempts to make implicit assumptions explicit and to reason about systems in terms of structure, incentives, and durability.

They are shared publicly in case they are useful to others thinking about similar problems.
